## 1. Terminology: caste vs task group

- **Caste (physical or morphological caste):** a body form fixed at development. In P. badius the castes are the **queen** (a **gyne** is an unmated winged female), the **male**, and two worker subcastes: the **minor worker** and the **major worker**. Ant biologists say P. badius has **dimorphic** workers. Gordon (1984 Psyche) calls it "the only polymorphic species in its genus", and its minors also vary continuously in size.
- **Task group / role / "temporal caste":** what a worker is doing *now*. It changes with age and colony need. Kwapich & Tschinkel (2013) loosely call these "behavioral castes". Gordon uses "task groups" or "activities". The process of changing tasks with age is **age polyethism (temporal polyethism)**.
- **There is no true "soldier" caste.** P. badius majors (large heads, about 5–7% of workers, rising to about 10% in July) are **not defenders**. Wheeler (1910, via EDIS) found them no more aggressive than minors, and they rarely leave the nest (Gordon 1984 Psyche). Their measured job is **seed milling**: with majors present, colonies opened more small and medium seeds, but the largest seed size opened stayed the same. Majors also have about twice the ovarioles of minors and are the main layers of male eggs when a colony loses its queen. **P. badius.**
- **"Scout" is not used for P. badius.** In P. barbatus the scouting job is called **patrolling** (Gordon). Gordon (1984 Psyche, via abstract) also recorded patrollers in **laboratory** P. badius colonies, where patrollers and midden workers were the same ants. Gordon (1984 Ecol Entomol, local) saw more patrolling and defense when other ant species invaded P. badius mounds. Field P. badius studies (Kwapich, Tschinkel) do not describe a patroller group. Treat patrolling as present in P. badius, but its share of workers is **Couldn't find it**.
- **"Repletes" do not exist in Pogonomyrmex.** Repletes are honeypot ants (e.g. *Myrmecocystus*) that store liquid in their crops. P. badius stores **seeds in granary chambers** 30–100 cm deep. No source mentions repletes in this genus, which is consistent with their absence; this is not a positive statement from a source.

## 3. Task groups (roles)

### 3.1 Callow (newly eclosed worker)
- **Caste:** minor or major. **Age:** 0 to a few weeks; the pale cuticle darkens with age (Kwapich & Tschinkel 2013 used a color scale).
- **Share of workers:** depends on season. New callows appear from late May or June; no new callows after October. **P. badius.**
- **Where:** deepest chambers, next to the brood (Tschinkel 1998, 1999).
- **Behavior:** carries large fat stores (callows average about 30% fat vs 23% in dark workers; Tschinkel 1998). Callows probably do brood care. In lab colonies, callows were most often seen **convening** (Gordon 1984 Psyche, via abstract).
- **Transition:** with age they move upward and lose fat.

### 3.2 Brood-care workers (nurses) and queen attendants
- **Caste:** minors (young). **Age:** from emergence to about 3–5 weeks for summer-born workers (inferred from the 43 days to first foraging, minus time as a transfer worker). This timing is my inference, not a measurement.
- **Share:** below 70 cm depth, over 90% of workers are unmarked non-foragers, "probably involved mostly in brood care" (Tschinkel & Hanley 2017). A whole-colony nurse percentage: **Couldn't find it**.
- **Where:** deepest chambers, where about 98% of brood is kept (less than 2% near the surface).
- **Triggers:** being near brood and the queen. Moving up in the nest is linked to age; the CO₂ gradient is a possible depth cue (Tschinkel). **P. badius.**

### 3.3 Seed millers (seed opening / processing)
- **Caste:** majors (and minors). **Where:** seed chambers 30–100 cm deep and brood chambers. Seeds narrower than about 1.4 mm can be opened; larger seeds must germinate in storage first (Seeds-Diet-Sizes.md; Kwapich et al. beetle paper citing Martyn et al. 2023).
- **Behavior:** majors raise the milling rate for small and medium seeds. Husks go to the midden. **P. badius.** Share of workers milling: **Couldn't find it**.

### 3.4 Nest-maintenance workers (excavation)
- **Inside the nest, P. badius:** sand is carried upward in stages by different groups, and about 2.5% of the sand stays below ground (Tschinkel et al. 2015 PLoS ONE, via abstract). Nest moves happen about once a year, mostly in May–November; most digging is finished within about 2 weeks.
- **Outside the nest:** in P. barbatus, nest-maintenance workers have the **weakest task fidelity** and switch to foraging or patrolling (Gordon 1989, via abstract). New nest-maintenance workers are recruited from inactive workers inside the nest (Gordon 1989). In lab P. badius, nest-maintenance workers later became patrollers (Gordon 1984 Psyche). **BORROWED (P. barbatus)**, plus P. badius lab data.

### 3.5 Transfer workers (P. badius-specific)
- **Defined by:** Tschinkel et al. 2015 and Tschinkel & Hanley 2017 (full text read).
- **Caste:** minors. **Age:** younger than foragers. Marked transfer workers later showed up as foragers.
- **Share:** **about 25% of the nest population in summer** (foragers about 40%).
- **Where:** they move throughout the vertical nest. About 30% were found below 20 cm (some at 110–120 cm). In the 30–80 cm seed-chamber zone they make up 20–40% of chamber occupants; deeper than about 70 cm, about 6% (range 0.04–10.3%). About 25% were found on the surface (possibly sand carriers, or mismarked foragers).
- **Behavior:** foragers drop seeds and insects in the top 10–15 cm. Transfer workers carry them down "in increments", visible as a "wave" of seeds, into the seed chambers (20–80 cm) and to the larvae. They also carry excavated sand up. The work is split into steps; each worker usually does only part of a multi-step chain. **P. badius.**

### 3.6 Foragers
- **Caste:** minors. **Age at first foraging:** about 43 days (SD 1.78) for summer-born workers; 200–360 days for autumn-born workers. Workers become foragers when their fat drops below about 10%.
- **Share:** 0 in winter; peaks around 37–40% in summer. Rule of thumb: foragers ≈ 1.6 × larvae (May–October).
- **Where:** above ground. Inside, 55% of marked foragers were on the surface, 40% in the top 20 cm, 5% below 20 cm. They stay in the top 12–15 cm and never go deeper than 10–15 cm.
- **Behavior:** solitary search from trunk trails, one seed per trip. They recruit with a poison-gland trail for large finds.
- **Timing:** March to November; forage from the morning until about 1.5 h before sunset; pause in extreme heat or heavy rain.
- **Time spent foraging:** mean 38 days (spring) or 26.9 days (after July); about 3.35–4% of foragers die per day.
- **Switching:** foraging is a **one-way final role**. Foragers did not go back to inside work even when there were extra larvae or they were penned and fed. Removing 50% of foragers was **not** made up by workers from other tasks, and larvae died as a result (K&T 2013, 2016). **P. badius.**
- **Contrast (BORROWED, P. barbatus):** nest-maintenance workers, patrollers and midden workers switch to foraging when extra food appears, and this switch is also irreversible (Gordon 1989; Gordon et al. 2005). Foragers leave faster when foragers return with food more often (antennal contact rate; Q33 in the facts file).

### 3.7 Patrollers (Brandon's "scouts")
- **P. barbatus (BORROWED):** each morning patrollers search the foraging area **before** foragers come out. Their safe return triggers foraging; if they do not return, foragers stay in. The best stimulus was one patroller return every 10 s; the cue is in cuticular hydrocarbons (Greene & Gordon 2007, via abstract, cited in the search). Patrollers also respond to disturbances and invaders. Workers recruited into patrolling tend to keep patrolling (Gordon 1989).
- **P. badius:** patrolling appears in lab colonies and is done by the **same ants as midden work**. These ants **died sooner** than other marked ants (Gordon 1984 Psyche, via abstract). In the field, patrolling and defense increase when other ant species invade (Gordon 1984 Ecol Entomol). Share and time of day for P. badius: **Couldn't find it**.

### 3.8 Midden workers
- **P. badius:** middens hold seed husks (mostly *Panicum*) and **charcoal** (millions of objects; about 200 g on a 0.6 m disc). Dead ants are carried up to 6 m away and are rarely put on the midden. Ants rebuild middens that are removed. When middens were removed, patrolling went up and nest maintenance went down (Gordon 1984 Ecol Entomol). Possible functions: a boundary or odor cue (Gordon 1984) or warming the soil (Smith & Tschinkel 2007). In the lab, midden work is done by the patroller group (Gordon 1984 Psyche).
- **Lab note (BORROWED, P. barbatus):** in lab colonies midden work is unusually common, and ants often do midden work just before they die (Gordon et al. 2005). Starting midden work depends on how often an ant meets midden workers (Gordon & Mehdiabadi 1999).

### 3.9 Guards / defenders
- **Who:** older workers at the surface ("as they appear at the surface, they change to guarding and foraging"; Tschinkel 1999). They are not majors. When a new object appears in the nest yard, ants gather around it and stand with mandibles open (Gordon 1984 Ecol Entomol).
- **Sting and venom:** Pogonomyrmex venoms are "the most toxic known from any of the Hymenoptera". North American Pogonomyrmex (20 species) have an LD50 of **0.12–0.7 mg/kg** (mice), and P. badius stings rate **3** on the Schmidt pain index ("Bold and unrelenting…"). The pain lasts 4–8 h (Schmidt 2019 Toxins, PMC6669698; Wikipedia's Schmidt index). I found no separate P. badius LD50 value. Schmidt & Blum 1978 (Toxicon 16:645) measured P. badius venom toxicity, but I read only the title and metadata, so no number is given here. Venom enzymes (P. badius): phospholipase A2 and B, hyaluronidase, lipase, acid phosphatase and esterases (Schmidt & Blum 1978, via abstract). The often-quoted figure for P. maricopa as the most toxic species: **not verified here**.
- **Alarm pheromone:** 4-methyl-3-heptanone from the mandibular gland. It attracts and excites workers within about 10 cm (Hölldobler & Wilson 1970, per the facts file). **P. badius.**
- **Recruitment:** a poison-gland trail laid by dragging the sting; 10–15 nestmates follow it; it fades in about 15–30 min. The Dufour's gland gives a longer-lasting orientation or home-range cue (Hölldobler & Wilson 1970). **P. badius.**
- **Fighting:** forager deaths come mostly from fights with neighboring colonies (K&T 2013, 2016).

### 3.10 Reserve / inactive workers
- **P. badius:** over 2 weeks, only about 10% of workers were active above ground (Golley & Gentry 1964, via abstract). The share idle at any one moment: **Couldn't find it**. The strongest P. badius "reserve" is physiological: autumn-born workers stay in the nest as fat reserves through winter. In this species, inside workers are not shifted into foraging when there is a shortage.
- **P. barbatus (BORROWED):** extra nest-maintenance workers come from previously inactive ants inside the nest (Gordon 1989).
- I could not confirm a "Kwapich, Valentine & Tschinkel 2018 worker reserves" paper. **Couldn't find it.**

### 3.11 Conveners (lab, P. badius / P. barbatus)
Small groups outside the nest that inspect and groom each other. In the lab they were mostly young callows and later became foragers (Gordon 1984 Psyche; Gordon 1986).

## 6. Sim notes
**Agent state machine (per minor worker):** `CALLOW → BROOD_CARE → TRANSFER (+ DIG) → [GUARD | PATROL/MIDDEN] → FORAGER → DEAD`. Majors: `MILLER` (inside the nest) and `REPRO_LAYER` (only when the queen has been dead for 28–35 days).
- **Moving up a state:** use age plus fat (fat falls over time; becomes a forager when fat < 0.10). Summer-born workers reach FORAGER at about 43 days. Autumn-born workers stay inside until the next March–July. Depth follows state: brood care below 70 cm, transfer at 20–80 cm, forager at 0–15 cm.
- **No going back:** never return from FORAGER. Do not move inside workers into foraging when foragers are lost (P. badius). As an option, a "barbatus mode" can allow switching into foraging based on encounter rate.
- **Starting a forage trip:** first patrollers (optional); then departure rate driven by forager returns (α model, Q33); only when temperature and daylight allow.
- **Transfer jobs:** a seed or sand pile at the top chamber creates a demand signal. Transfer workers move items down (or up) one chamber band at a time, with relay hand-offs.
- **Defense:** an intruder or disturbance releases alarm pheromone (about 10 cm radius). GUARD, PATROL and FORAGER ants within range swarm it and sting. Majors do not respond.
- **Recruitment:** large prey triggers a poison-gland trail (half-life about 10–15 min) and 10–15 recruits.
- **Starting ratios (summer):** FORAGER 0.40, TRANSFER 0.25, inside 0.35; majors 0.05–0.10 of all workers; foragers ≈ 1.6 × larvae. In winter, FORAGER is 0.
- **Death:** foragers die at about 0.0335–0.04 per day; non-foragers rarely die. In the lab, foragers live much longer (up to 8×), so a "lab mode" should lower forager mortality and raise midden work (Gordon et al. 2005).
